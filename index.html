Proving Income for a Digital Nomad Visa: Employer Letters vs Freelance Contracts

Every digital nomad visa sets an income threshold, but the paperwork used to prove it looks very different depending on whether the applicant is a salaried employee or a freelancer with several clients. Programs were largely designed around the employee case first, which means freelancers often have to work harder to assemble an equivalent paper trail. Nomad Desk's Japan digital nomad visa guide and Portugal D8 briefing both spell out what their programs specifically accept.

The employee path: one letter usually does most of the work

For salaried remote employees, the core document is typically an employer letter confirming the role, salary, remote-work arrangement, and that the position can be performed from abroad. Paired with a few months of payslips and a bank statement showing the salary landing, this is often enough to satisfy the income requirement. The main risk on this path is timing — some programs want the employer letter dated within a narrow window before submission, so requesting it too early can mean redoing it later.

The freelance path: more documents, more explaining

Freelancers and business owners generally need to show a consistent income pattern rather than a single confirming letter. That usually means several months of client invoices, corresponding bank deposits, and sometimes a summary letter from an accountant. Income that fluctuates month to month — common for freelance work — can trigger extra scrutiny, so it often helps to show an average over a longer period (six months to a year) rather than just the most recent, possibly slow, month. Programs that specify a minimum number of income sources or clients should be checked carefully, since a single-client freelancer can sometimes be treated more like an employee than a diversified freelancer.

Currency and threshold interpretation differs by program

Income thresholds are usually published in local currency or USD, but how exchange rate fluctuations are handled at the time of application is not always explained clearly. Applying when a threshold is only just met is riskier than applying with a comfortable margin, given exchange rates can move between document preparation and review. This is one more reason to build in slack, similar to timing an application around a destination's best-time-to-visit window rather than the exact minimum requirement.

Keep documents current through the review period

Visa review can take weeks to months depending on the country, and some programs request updated bank statements if too much time passes between submission and a decision. Staying reachable during this period matters — an eSIM plan with reliable data makes it easier to respond quickly to a request for additional documents, whether the destination is Thailand or Mexico.

Practical takeaways

  • Employees: get an employer letter dated close to submission, plus recent payslips and bank statements.
  • Freelancers: gather invoices and deposits over several months, not just the most recent one.
  • Apply with a comfortable margin above the minimum threshold, not exactly at it.
  • Keep documents ready to refresh in case a review takes longer than expected.

This is general information, not legal or immigration advice — requirements vary by program and change over time, so confirm against an official source before applying.
